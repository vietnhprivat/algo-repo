\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Motivation}

Day-ahead electricity price forecasting (EPF) is the task of predicting the
wholesale price of electricity for each delivery period of the following day in
a given bidding zone, from a combination of historical prices and exogenous
signals such as load and renewable-generation forecasts. Accurate day-ahead
forecasts are essential to generators, retailers, and grid operators, who rely
on them to schedule production, hedge positions, and bid into the day-ahead
auction. The task has grown harder over the past decade. The expansion of wind
and solar generation introduces supply-side volatility that statistical models
struggle to capture, and the coupling of European bidding zones propagates price
shocks across borders in ways historical data may not reflect. The resulting
price series are heavy-tailed and spike-prone. Under high renewable infeed they
increasingly admit negative prices, a regime that rules out modelling choices
that presume positivity, such as a logarithmic price transform
\cite{weron2014}.

\section{The gap between forecasting research and operation}

Research on EPF has produced increasingly accurate models, spanning statistical,
reduced-form, and machine-learning approaches, and the field has been surveyed in
depth \cite{weron2014, nowotarski2018}. The open-access benchmark of Lago et al.\
\cite{lago2021} was an important step. It documented how severe the evaluation
problem had become. New methods were compared on private datasets, over test
periods too short to be conclusive, and against baselines too weak to be
informative. In response, Lago et al.\ standardised both a pair of benchmark
models and a methodology for comparing them.

Yet even the most careful EPF contributions stop at the boundary of offline
evaluation. They standardise how models are \emph{compared}, but not how they
are \emph{run}. Ingesting data on a schedule, versioning experiments, detecting
model degradation, and retraining on fresh data are left unaddressed. This gap
between building a model and operating it is the one that Machine Learning
Operations (MLOps) emerged to close, by carrying DevOps principles such as
automation, reproducibility, and monitoring into machine-learning systems.
Zarour et al.\ \cite{zarour2025} synthesise the MLOps literature into a
systematic review of nine core practices and an accompanying maturity model. The
two bodies of work rarely meet. EPF research treats forecasting as a problem
that ends when predictions are produced, while MLOps research is largely
domain-agnostic. The closest precedent for joining them is the work of
Subramanya et al.\ \cite{subramanya2022devops2mlops}, who apply an MLOps pipeline
to electricity-market forecasting as a case study.

\section{Aim and research questions}

This thesis takes the benchmark EPF models from the forecasting literature and
embeds them in an end-to-end production platform built along MLOps principles.
The platform ingests data from the ENTSO-E Transparency Platform on a recurring
schedule, stores it in ClickHouse, produces day-ahead forecasts through
rolling-window recalibration, monitors prediction quality and data drift, and
maintains a complete audit trail from every forecast back to the code version
and data window that produced it. It is evaluated on two axes: the \emph{forecast
quality} of the models it serves, and the \emph{operational maturity} of the
system that serves them. Three research questions structure the investigation.

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1 (forecasting).] How do faithfully replicated LEAR, DNN, and
    Transformer day-ahead EPF models compare on recent ENTSO-E data for the DK1
    and DE bidding zones, when set against one another, a weekly-naive baseline,
    and the rankings reported by Lago et al.\ \cite{lago2021} and Llorente and
    Portela \cite{llorente2025transformer}?
  \item[RQ2 (engineering).] What engineering trade-offs and operational
    constraints emerge when embedding these benchmark models into a scheduled,
    containerised, continuously monitored production pipeline?
  \item[RQ3 (maturity).] Assessed against the nine MLOps practices of Zarour et
    al.\ \cite{zarour2025} and the Google maturity model, which practices does
    the platform realise, which prove most critical, and where does a
    single-host minimum-viable platform reach its limit?
\end{description}

\section{Contributions}

This work makes four contributions.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A production EPF platform.} An end-to-end system that ingests
    live ENTSO-E data, recalibrates and forecasts on a daily schedule, monitors
    data and model health, and records the full provenance of every run. This is
    the artefact that RQ2 and RQ3 are evaluated against.
  \item \textbf{A faithful model replication.} LEAR and the DNN of Lago et al.\
    \cite{lago2021} and the Transformer of Llorente and Portela
    \cite{llorente2025transformer}, reimplemented to specification and compared
    across two markets on a shared recent test window with paired
    Diebold--Mariano and Giacomini--White significance tests (RQ1).
  \item \textbf{A composed quantitative MLOps evaluation.} A combination of the
    ML Test Score rubric, provenance-coverage measurement, and a worked
    reproducibility re-run, assembled to give the otherwise-qualitative maturity
    question a measurable answer.
  \item \textbf{A practice-by-practice maturity assessment} of the platform
    against Zarour et al.'s nine practices, with each practice traced to a
    concrete repository artefact and positioned on the Google maturity model.
\end{itemize}

The scope is deliberately bounded. The work forecasts point prices, not
probabilistic distributions. It is a single-case study of one platform, so its
system-side findings support analytic rather than statistical generalisation.
Because the source papers' test periods predate the present price regime, the
forecasting comparison is framed in relative terms (rMAE and rankings) and not as
a comparison of absolute errors.

\section{Report structure}

Chapter~\ref{ch:background} reviews the EPF and MLOps literatures and the
evaluation methodology this work draws on. Chapter~\ref{ch:methods-models}
specifies the three forecasting models, their features, the recalibration
scheme, and the metrics and significance tests used to compare them.
Chapter~\ref{ch:system-implementation} documents the production system around
those models, organised around Zarour's nine practices.
Chapter~\ref{ch:results-models} reports the forecasting comparison, and
Chapter~\ref{ch:results-system} sets out how the system is evaluated and reports
the result. Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} answers the three research questions and
