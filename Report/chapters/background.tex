\chapter{Background and Related Work}
\label{ch:background}

This chapter reviews the two literatures the thesis joins, electricity price
forecasting and MLOps, together with the evaluation methodology it borrows
from empirical software engineering. Section~\ref{sec:bg-epf} covers day-ahead
EPF and its statistical character. Section~\ref{sec:bg-lago} introduces the
open-access benchmark that anchors the modelling thread.
Section~\ref{sec:bg-transformer} describes the Transformer approach that supplies
the third model. Section~\ref{sec:bg-mlops} sets out the MLOps practices and
maturity models behind the system thread. Section~\ref{sec:bg-eval} lists the
instruments used to evaluate the platform.

\section{Day-ahead electricity price forecasting}
\label{sec:bg-epf}

In a day-ahead market, participants submit bids for each delivery period of the
following day and a uniform clearing price is set, once per day, at the
intersection of aggregated supply and demand within each bidding zone. The
clearing price is set by the marginal generator, so it inherits the cost
structure of the generation mix and the topology of cross-border exchange.
This mechanism produces a price series with pronounced multi-scale seasonality
(daily, weekly, and annual), heavy tails, and sudden spikes driven by scarcity
or congestion. As wind and solar capacity has grown, two further features have
become routine: sharp drops, and outright negative prices, when inflexible
must-run generation coincides with high renewable infeed and low demand. Weron's
review \cite{weron2014} surveys the statistical and computational-intelligence
methods developed for this problem, and Nowotarski and Weron
\cite{nowotarski2018} extend the picture to probabilistic forecasting. Both
emphasise that the EPF methodology, and not only the choice of model, determines
whether reported gains are real.

\section{The Lago et al.\ open-access benchmark}
\label{sec:bg-lago}

Lago et al.\ \cite{lago2021} address the reproducibility problem in EPF directly,
contributing an open-access benchmark comprising five market datasets (Nord
Pool, PJM, and the EPEX zones BE, FR, and DE), two reference models, and the
\texttt{epftoolbox} library that standardises data splits and evaluation. The
first reference model, LEAR (Lasso Estimated AutoRegressive), fits an
$L^1$-regularised linear model over a parameter-rich feature space of price lags,
exogenous forecasts, and calendar dummies. The Lasso performs feature selection
and coefficient estimation jointly, which keeps the rich input space tractable.
The second is a deep neural network, a multi-layer perceptron whose
architecture and inputs are tuned per dataset. Despite its simplicity, LEAR
matched or outperformed the deep alternative when evaluated over long test
periods, which is why it serves as the modelling foundation of this thesis.

Two methodological contributions of the benchmark are reused here directly.
First, models are recalibrated on a rolling window and their forecasts averaged
across an ensemble of window lengths, rather than trained once. Second, the
benchmark argues for the relative mean absolute error (rMAE), which normalises
the model's error by that of a naive baseline so that results remain comparable
across periods and markets, and for formal significance testing of accuracy
differences via the Diebold--Mariano \cite{diebold1995dm} and multivariate
Giacomini--White \cite{giacomini2006gw} tests. The benchmark also adopts the
inverse-hyperbolic-sine (asinh) variance-stabilising transform of Uniejewski et
al.\ \cite{uniejewski2018vst}, which compresses spikes while remaining defined
for negative prices.

\section{Transformer approaches to EPF}
\label{sec:bg-transformer}

Attention-based architectures have more recently been applied to EPF. Llorente
and Portela \cite{llorente2025transformer} propose an encoder-only Transformer.
It takes two inputs, a sequence of the previous days' prices and the day-ahead
exogenous forecasts. It embeds and attends over the price sequence, then combines
the result with the exogenous representation through a final multi-layer
perceptron. Benchmarked on the same open-access datasets, it provides the third
model family compared in this work. The authors evaluate without retraining
inside the test period, applying only a renormalisation step between days. This
thesis instead places the Transformer in the same rolling-recalibration pipeline
as the other models, a deviation documented in Chapter~\ref{ch:methods-models}.

\section{MLOps: practices and maturity}
\label{sec:bg-mlops}

MLOps applies DevOps principles such as automation, version control, continuous
delivery, and monitoring to the particular demands of machine-learning
systems, where data and models, not only code, evolve over time. Zarour et al.\
\cite{zarour2025} consolidate the field in a systematic literature review that
identifies nine recurring best practices: automating CI/CD, orchestrating
workflows, ensuring reproducibility, versioning, collaboration and
communication, continuously training and evaluating models, tracking and logging
ML metadata, continuous monitoring, and implementing feedback loops. These nine
practices provide the organising structure for the system chapter
(Chapter~\ref{ch:system-implementation}).

The same review surveys five MLOps maturity models, all descended from the
CMM/CMMI lineage: the Google three-level model, the Microsoft and GigaOm
five-level models, the four-level model of John, Olsson and Bosch, and the
five-level model of Mateo-Casal\'i et al. This work positions the platform
against the Google model \cite{google_mlops_maturity}, whose three levels (manual
process, ML-pipeline automation, and CI/CD-pipeline automation) match the
granularity of a single-host platform. That choice is justified in
Chapter~\ref{ch:results-system}. The closest domain application is Subramanya et
al.\ \cite{subramanya2022devops2mlops}, who build an MLOps pipeline for an
electricity-market forecasting task and evaluate it as a case study. This is the
precedent this thesis follows in evaluating its own platform in its real
operating context.

\section{Evaluating machine-learning systems}
\label{sec:bg-eval}

Because the platform is a single artefact rather than a sample, its system-side
evaluation follows the single-case study methodology of Runeson and H\"ost
\cite{runeson2009casestudy}. That method supports \emph{analytic} generalisation,
extending findings to cases that share the platform's characteristics, rather
than statistical generalisation to a population. Within that frame, several
established instruments are composed. The ML Test Score of Breck et al.\
\cite{breck2017mltestscore} is a 28-item rubric across four sections (data,
model development, ML infrastructure, and monitoring) whose final score is the
minimum of the four section sums, so the weakest area bounds the result.
Reproducibility is treated in two senses. Gundersen and Kjensmo
\cite{gundersen2018reproducibility} characterise degrees of reproducibility in
AI research. Finally, the decision of whether drift should
trigger retraining is informed by the cost-aware analysis of Mahadevan and
Mathioudakis \cite{mahadevan2024costaware}, who show that triggered retraining
earns its keep mainly when retraining is expensive. How these instruments are
applied, and why each was chosen, is the subject of
Chapter~\ref{ch:results-system}.
